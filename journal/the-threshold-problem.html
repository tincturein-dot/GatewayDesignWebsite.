The threshold problem

26 July 2026 The Gateway Designs Delhi

A homepage gets about four seconds to say where someone has landed. Most of them spend those seconds proving the company can afford a stock photo.

Watch someone open a site they have never seen before. They do not read. They sweep — top left, across, down the middle, out. Somewhere in that sweep they decide whether this place is for them, and the decision is mostly made before the hero video has finished buffering. Everything after that is either confirmation or a back button.

Which makes the top of a homepage the most expensive real estate in a brand's whole presence, and the part most likely to have been filled in by a template.

What a doorway actually does

A real doorway does three things at once. It tells you where you are, it hints at what is inside, and it gives you one obvious way through. You have never stood in front of a door wondering what it is for.

Hold most homepages to that standard and they fail on all three. The headline is a mood rather than a statement. What is inside is described in categories — solutions, platform, services — that could belong to any company in any industry. And there are four ways in, all styled with equal weight, which is the same as having none.

A wall can be beautiful. It is still a wall.

The enumeration habit

This is not a taste problem, it is a process problem. Templates are lists of slots: hero, logo bar, three feature cards, testimonial, stats, call to action. Each slot asks a question — what goes here? — and the question is nearly impossible not to answer. Nobody leaves a slot empty. So the page fills up with material that exists because there was room for it.

The result is a page that describes a company to someone who has not yet decided to care. Every section is defensible on its own. Together they are a wall.

Working from the entrance backwards is a different exercise. You start with one question — what does this person need in order to take one more step — and you earn every element after that. Sections do not get to exist because a layout has a gap.

Say the plain thing first

Cleverness costs a beat, and at the top of the page you cannot afford one. "We design and build websites for brands in India" is a worse sentence than almost anything a copywriter would prefer, and it outperforms all of them, because a stranger can act on it immediately.

The pattern to watch for is the headline that would survive if you swapped in a competitor's logo. Crafting digital experiences that inspire. Where strategy meets creativity. These are not claims, they are ambience. If the sentence is true of four hundred other companies, it is doing none of your work.

Put the plain sentence first and the ambitious one second. The second line is where you get to have a voice, because by then the reader knows what they are looking at and is willing to spend attention on how you say it.

One door, ranked

Two calls to action with identical styling do not double your options, they hand a stranger a decision they have no basis to make. Pick the one thing you want most and let it look like the answer. The second option can be there, quieter, for the person who already knows what they want.

The same discipline applies to navigation. Six top-level items styled identically is a filing cabinet, not a route. Most visitors want one of two paths — see the work, or start a conversation. Design for those two and let everything else be findable rather than prominent.

Weight is part of the welcome

None of this survives a slow page. Google's own threshold for Largest Contentful Paint is 2.5 seconds, and that is measured at the 75th percentile of real visits — not on your laptop, on the office wifi, with everything warm in cache.

We build for a country where a lot of those visits arrive on a mid-range Android phone with two bars of signal, and it changes decisions. A hero video is a real thing to want, and it is also several megabytes ahead of the headline in the queue. Web fonts are worth paying for, and each additional weight is another file blocking the first paint. So: one display weight preloaded, everything else deferred. A poster frame that carries the composition on its own, so the video is an upgrade rather than a requirement. Motion that starts after the text has arrived, not instead of it.

A door that takes four seconds to open is a wall with extra steps.

The read-aloud test

Here is the whole thing in one exercise, and it costs nothing. Read the top of your homepage out loud to someone who does not know your company — a friend, a relative, anyone outside the industry. Stop at the fold. Then ask them two questions: what do these people do, and what would you do next.

If they hesitate on either, the problem is not the design of the page. It is that the page was built as a wall, and you have been asking people to climb it.

We build sites from the entrance backwards — structure first, then art direction, then the last line of production code. If your homepage is a wall, tell us where you want people to go.
